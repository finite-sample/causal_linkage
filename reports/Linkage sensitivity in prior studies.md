# Studies already demonstrate causal linkage sensitivity

**Yes: researchers have shown that linkage choices change estimated effects, uncertainty, and statistical conclusions—including in causal analyses.** There are direct empirical sensitivity analyses, simulations explaining the mechanisms, and methods that optimize downstream statistics over sets of possible matchings. The closest examples also anticipate our Rajasthan audit's qualification: dramatic sensitivity can depend on implausibly permissive linkage assumptions. This establishes that the phenomenon is documented; it does not establish that checking it is routine across applied studies. Nor do the studies below establish that a few credible near-tie substitutions would overturn the Rajasthan findings.

Research checked: 8 October 2026. The comparison distinguishes changing linkage algorithms or samples, varying assumptions about linkage errors, and optimizing over a constrained set of complete linkages.

| Study | What changes | What it establishes | Important limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shan, Thomas & Gutman (2021) | Outcome dependence in treatment-roster linkage | A causal mortality estimate and interval can change substantially | Extreme settings override identifier disagreements; authors judge them implausible |
| Harron et al. (2017) | Linkage procedure and acceptance threshold | Estimated associations and significance change | Linked sample also changes; significance can move through lost precision |
| Wortman & Reiter (2018) | Outcome-linkage threshold | Causal bias and variance depend on linkage choices and error location | Simulation; not fixed-cohort injective bounds |
| Tahamont et al. (2021) | Administrative outcome ascertainment and linkage method | Linkage errors affect experimental treatment estimates and power | Theory and simulation using real identifiers, not a real trial's significance reversal |
| Hall & Fienberg (2012) | Feasible bipartite matching | Regression extrema over matching confidence sets are explicitly formulated | Regression implementation uses approximation and a greedy search |
| Turkcapar & Krishnan (2023) | Candidate graph and matching restrictions | Similarity rules materially alter aggregate bounds | Aggregate queries, not causal tests |

**A real causal application contains an especially close precedent.** Shan, Thomas, and Gutman link a Meals on Wheels participant roster to Medicare records, so identity uncertainty affects treatment membership. They explicitly vary outcome-dependent linkage: a multiplier favors candidate links associated with death within 30 days. The estimated mortality ATT risk difference is **0.008, with 95% interval [−0.067, 0.083]**, at the neutral multiplier of 1; **0.086 [0.002, 0.170]** at 50; and **0.549 [0.412, 0.686]** at 100. These are risk differences on the proportion scale. Crucially, the authors judge the extreme settings implausible because they can override substantial disagreement in identifying fields. Their separate blocking sensitivity produces much steadier mortality estimates, 0.005–0.010, with all intervals crossing zero. This directly illustrates why a dramatic sensitivity result needs a substantive assessment of the alternatives producing it. It is not evidence about a small number of independently plausible name substitutions. ([Shan et al., Sections 3.4 and 5, Tables 4 and 8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9222523/))

**Applied comparisons also show ordinary linkage choices changing significance.** Harron et al. compare mother–baby linkage procedures. For neonatal survival among mothers with versus without delivery risk factors, adjusted odds ratios and p-values are: gold standard **0.40, p=.039**; probabilistic linkage **0.42, p=.044**; stricter probabilistic linkage **0.35, p=.011**; deterministic linkage **0.52, p=.143**. The deterministic procedure links fewer than half the records. Thus the last comparison partly reflects precision loss, rather than a reversal in estimated direction. Another comparison—delivery risk factors among Black versus White mothers—moves from **OR .98, p=.700** under the gold standard to **OR .80, p=.017** under deterministic linkage. The paper diagnoses selection from differential missed matches. These are adjusted associations, not established causal effects. ([Harron et al., Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5837697/))

Historical economics provides evidence in both directions. Bailey et al.'s final published abstract reports attenuation of intergenerational income elasticity by up to **29%**, and more under common linkage variations. Abramitzky et al., however, find similar substantive parameters across several plausible linkage methods and recommend repeating analyses across alternative linked samples. The lesson is setting dependence: linkage sensitivity is neither an undiscovered concern nor an inevitable dramatic result. These comparisons generally change which observations are linked as well as how they are linked. ([Bailey et al., 2020](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.20191526); [Abramitzky et al., 2021](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.20201599))

**The causal-methods literature goes beyond maximizing match precision.** Wortman and Reiter vary outcome-linkage thresholds and examine both treatment estimates and their estimated variances. They distinguish harmless within-treatment, within-subclass outcome swaps from errors crossing those boundaries; regression adjustment can change the invariance. Their simulations also show that smaller reported variance need not mean better inference. In one nonlinear scenario, a minimum-estimated-variance rule gives empirical variance **29.2** but average estimated variance only **5.8**, alongside bias. Their simulation permits duplicate donor use and threshold-dependent sample sizes, so it is not the same as our injective fixed-cohort uncertainty set. ([Wortman & Reiter, Sections 3 and 5, Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/1709.03631))

Tahamont et al.'s *Dude, Where's My Treatment Effect?* directly studies linked administrative outcomes in randomized experiments. It derives consequences for treatment estimates, standard errors, and power, and compares exact with probabilistic linkage using simulations built from Oregon court identifiers. Stringent matching can discard outcome events and damage power. This is a direct antecedent to the broad causal concern, although its empirical component is a simulation rather than a real randomized experiment whose conclusion is reversed. ([Tahamont et al., 2021, theory and empirical-simulation sections](https://ccjs.umd.edu/sites/ccjs.umd.edu/files/pubs/Tahamont2021_Article_DudeWhereSMyTreatmentEffectErr.pdf))

**Even optimizing over feasible linkages has direct predecessors.** Hall and Fienberg explicitly define minimum and maximum regression statistics over a confidence set of bipartite matchings. They distinguish uncertainty about the statistic under the unknown correct matching from uncertainty about a population parameter, which requires sampling uncertainty too. Their particular regression implementation approximates the changing design matrix and uses a greedy algorithm without guaranteed exact extrema. Nevertheless, the general idea of bounding downstream regression over matching sets is clearly present. ([Hall & Fienberg, 2012, Section 5](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rjhall/linkage.pdf))

Turkcapar and Krishnan show something particularly relevant to our audit: aggregate bounds over candidate matchings depend strongly on the similarity rule and candidate restrictions. Poor candidate precision widens bounds; poor recall can exclude the true answer. Their experiments compare similarity measures and show wider intervals from an unsuitable measure. This is aggregate-query research, but the substantive warning transfers: **narrower bounds are useful only if the restrictions retain credible true links**. ([Turkcapar & Krishnan, 2023, Sections 5.3–5.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.05178v1))

Related causal sensitivity research also asks how few binary outcome corrections could reverse a randomization-test decision, in either direction. Heng and Shaw formulate this through integer optimization and explicitly distinguish worst-case vulnerability from evidence that those errors actually occurred. Their unknowns are outcome labels, not one-to-one identities, but the interpretation closely matches the distinction we need. ([Heng & Shaw, 2025, Sections 3–4](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.03111v3))

For Rajasthan, the useful question is therefore whether **independently defensible identity alternatives** produce consequential changes while holding the analysis otherwise fixed. Our permissive graph demonstrates conditional mathematical sensitivity; the tighter diagnostics show how strongly that answer depends on graph construction. Neither validates the identity set. The targeted search did not locate our exact combination of a fixed cohort, candidate-constrained injections, changed-link budget, and stratified-lottery p-value analysis. That bounded finding does not establish novelty. The immediate value is a careful application with transparent feasible-set justification, building on an established sensitivity-analysis literature.
